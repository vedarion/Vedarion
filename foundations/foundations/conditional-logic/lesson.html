Conditional Logic: The Foundation

Valid inferences, the contrapositive, and the two traps to avoid.

What you'll learn

Conditional logic is the backbone of LSAT Logical Reasoning. This lesson covers the if-then relationship, how to diagram it, and the exact inferences you can and cannot draw.

Key Takeaways

✓A → B — the original conditional is always valid.
✓/B → /A — the contrapositive is always valid.
✗/A → ??? — negating the sufficient gives you nothing.
✗B → ??? — affirming the necessary gives you nothing.

Apply what you learned

1
"All surgeons must complete a residency."
Indicator: All … must
▾
Diagram: Identify sufficient & necessary, draw the arrow.Reveal
"All" introduces the sufficient. So surgeon is sufficient. "Must" introduces the necessary. So complete a residency is necessary.
Surgeon → Complete Residency
Part A: Someone is a surgeon. Conclude?Reveal
✓ They completed a residency.
Surgeon is sufficient — when triggered, the necessary follows.
Surgeon → Complete Residency ✓
Part B: Someone did NOT complete a residency. Conclude?Reveal
✓ They are NOT a surgeon.
Residency is required. Failing it means they can't be a surgeon.
/Complete Residency → /Surgeon ✓
Part C: Someone completed a residency. Conclude?Reveal
✗ Nothing.
Lots of doctors complete residencies without becoming surgeons.
Complete Residency → ??? ✗
Part D: Someone is NOT a surgeon. Conclude?Reveal
✗ Nothing.
They might have completed a residency or might not.
/Surgeon → ??? ✗

The Four Scenarios

If sufficientA → B✓ Valid
If not necessary/B → /A✓ Valid
If necessaryB → ???✗ Trap
If not sufficient/A → ???✗ Trap
Remember: The only two valid inferences are the original conditional and the contrapositive. Everything else is a trap. When in doubt, ask yourself: "Am I triggering the sufficient, or negating the necessary?" If yes, you can conclude. If not, you can't.
