Pattern Recognition

Cost-Benefit

The conclusion judges the whole decision, but the premises put only one side of the ledger on the scale. You would need the other side to know whether it holds.

Lesson Cheat Sheet

The pattern

The author names a cost or a benefit, then concludes whether something is good, bad, worth it, or will pay off, without weighing the other side of the ledger.

Partial premises. A whole-picture conclusion.

Two forms

Cost-only

Names a downside, then concludes the whole thing is bad or not worth it.

Benefit-only

Names an upside, then concludes the whole thing is good or will pay off.

Two examples

Running

PremiseRunning causes knee strain.
ConclusionTherefore, running is bad for you.

The gapOne cost named. No benefit weighed: cardio, longevity, mood, weight management.

Machine X

PremiseMachine X cools the apartment and is cheap to install.
ConclusionTherefore, we will save on our electrical bill.

The gapTwo benefits noted. The operating cost, how much electricity Machine X actually uses, was never weighed.

How to spot it

The premise names a single cost or benefit. The conclusion jumps to a bigger judgment: good, bad, worth it, will pay off, the right call.

Premise covers a slice. Conclusion covers the whole.

At the answer stage

You cannot pre-list every cost and benefit the argument missed, so do not try. Stay flexible, and ask one question of the answer in front of you:

Should the argument have considered this cost or benefit before drawing its conclusion?

If yes, that is your answer.

Pattern

Partial cost-benefit premises, a whole-picture conclusion.

Strategy

Stay flexible. Ask whether the missing cost or benefit should have been weighed.
