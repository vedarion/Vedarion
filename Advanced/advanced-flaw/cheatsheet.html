Advanced Flaw

The first few words of the answer choice tell you which test to use.

Three types of answer choices
DESCRIPTIVELY ACCURATEconcludes, infers, argues, treats, confuses (not a complete list)
1
Is it descriptively accurate?
Every word of the answer choice has to match what happens in the stimulus.
2
Is it a flaw?
An answer can describe the argument perfectly and still be wrong if what it describes isn't a problem. You need a yes on both.
NECESSARY ASSUMPTIONassumes, presumes, takes for granted
1
Must be true test
Is every single word of the answer choice supported by the stimulus?
or
Negation test
Negate the answer, add it as a premise, and see if the conclusion falls apart. Either test does the job. You don't need to check whether it's unstated. It almost always is.
WEAKENfails to consider, ignores the possibility, overlooks
1
Did the argument leave it out?
If the argument addressed it, eliminate. If it didn't, don't call it out of scope. Being left out is the whole point. Go to step 2.
2
Add the fact to the argument. Does the conclusion get weaker?
It only has to weaken, not destroy. You may have to work with the answer. For example, with “whether or not X,” try it both ways. If either way weakens, the answer can work.
Watch out
Not every “fails to” is Weaken
“Fails to establish” and “provides no evidence” describe what the argument did, so use the Descriptively Accurate test. To check whether it's a flaw, ask: was the missing piece important? Would leaving it out weaken the argument?
Struggling with a Weaken answer?
Leave it alone. Don't eliminate it, and check the rest. On a hard question, it will be the one left, so you can be more confident it's right. On an easier one, another answer may be correct without the extra work.
Always ask: What do the first few words tell me to test?
