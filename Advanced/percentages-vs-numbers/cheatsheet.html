Percentages vs. Numbers

Three pieces, one formula, and the flaw the LSAT keeps testing.

The framework
%100PERCENT × TOTAL = PART
Know any two, and you can find the third. With only one, you can't.
Example1,000 students took an exam, and 30% passed.
Fill it in30/100 × 1,000 = 300 passed
What the LSAT does
It gives you one of the three pieces, then concludes about another. It never tells you the third.
That missing piece is the flaw. It can be any of the three. Three different arguments:
ARGUMENT 1
GIVENPercent
CONCLUDEDPart
MISSINGTotal
ARGUMENT 2
GIVENPercent
CONCLUDEDTotal
MISSINGPart
ARGUMENT 3
GIVENPart
CONCLUDEDPercent
MISSINGTotal
Keep in mind
Percent ≠ Number
Why
A percent tells you "out of." It never tells you "how many." To get how many, you need the total.
Trap: reading 30% as 30.
30% passed. How many?
1,000 took it300 passed
10,000 took it3,000 passed
Higher Percent ≠ Bigger Part
Why
Two groups can have very different totals, so the higher percent can still be the smaller part.
Trap: comparing percents as if the groups were the same size.
30% of students and 40% of teachers want recess. So more teachers do?
10,000 students3,000
1,000 teachers400
Rates
Why
A rate, like 10 per 1,000 people, is a percent. It already accounts for the size of the population. If the argument gives a rate and concludes about a rate or a chance, the total is not the flaw. Look somewhere else.
Trap: blaming a population change when the argument stays rate to rate.
Crime rate: 10 per 1,000 both years.
1,000 people10 crimes
2,000 people20 crimes
Your chance1 in 100 both years
Every time you see a percent, ask
1What did they give me?
2What did they conclude?
3What's missing?
Do I have two of the pieces? If not, look for the missing third.
